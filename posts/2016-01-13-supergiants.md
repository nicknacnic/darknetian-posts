Once the news broke out about the Panama Papers in April, people around the world began to question the legality of shell companies, disclosures, and tax aversion all taking place in countries dubbed as "tax havens."

These countries have pro-business tax codes which provide for companies to re-locate their headquarters abroad and pay only income tax in the U.S., nothing else.

Typically, in the countries abroad, taxes will be far less in some industries. Because of this, Apple moved their worldwide headquarters to Ireland, so any money made outside of the U.S. would face different income taxes.

The U.S. has around 39% corporate income tax, while Ireland has been dropping to single digits for decades now. It's obvious why Apple won't bring home an estimated $178 billion in cash from abroad... they will keep more this way!

Of the rumored $22 billion Apple made in 2011, only a mere $55 million was deemed taxable by the Irish authorities. The E.U. has decided to fine Apple a significant sum (~$17 billion) in back-taxes over the many years they have been avoiding the full payment.

Even with all this considered, Apple's share price fell less than 1% today. The fine they would pay isn't even 1/10th of the money they have in cash outside of the United States. At some point the people might want to ask themselves, is there such thing as a business being too big?

I personally have many Apple products, and are quite fond of them. I've installed the major operating systems on my macbook (running Linux and Windows, too), and have a number of features on my phone, as well. It's no surprise the company is giant, as the user interface is very easy to use.

As much as I love being able to work in bash and UNIX for my classes on these machines, I can't overlook the red flags Apple is being hit with today.

How is it possible that a company that has been rumored to pay .0005% of income taxes, having owed 6.5%, is only facing a fine of less than 1/10th of their reserves abroad? Not that I am implying Apple should face stricter punishment, I just don't see how the E.U. feels like they will be deterring this sort of behavior in the future.

To me that fine is laughable in the context of setting legal precedent.

The U.S. Treasury has requested the E.U. not pursue American companies over this sort of tax code grey area, however, it seems Amazon and Google are next in line for a slap on the wrist.

At some point I hope politicians with some real tact in economics come into office so questions of liability, disclosures, and tax code can not only be uniform and fair, but enforceable.
