Presidential elections always reveal popular public opinion of candidates, but never really the inner workings of the government, or the campaigns.

In 91', George H.W. Bush had the highest ratings of any president ever, at 88% approval. Yet, in 92' he lost to Bill Clinton, and became the 4th sitting president to not be re-elected. In 96' Bill carried only 4 Southern states, yet still won a decisive election (because foreigners donated to parties on both sides illegally). In 2000 and 2004 not a single Southern state voted Democrat. This was the 4th election in history when the popular vote did not outweigh the electoral college vote, and George W. Bush went into office. Although there were reported voter irregularities in Ohio, in 2004 Bush won again in a close contest over John Kerry. Surely after that recap, we can all recall the atmosphere and cultural standpoints in the 2008 and 2012 elections with Obama's wins over John McCain, and Mitt Romney, respectively.

All my life, I've been a part of "unprecedented" elections. The first I really remember having any opinions on was Obama/McCain. McCain would've been the oldest president ever, with the first female VP, Sarah Palin... and on the other side Obama would be the first African-American president with the first Roman Catholic vice president. After that, Obama versus the first Mormon candidate.

This election's "unprecedented" carries a different undertone than the "unprecedented" of elections past. Historically, the groundbreaking events of elections were always positive, or semi-positive occurrences. Highest presidential approval rate to lose demonstrated the ability of our system to change. Popular vote not outweighing electoral votes demonstrated the strength (or disorder) of our system. The previous two elections demonstrated the diversity and strength of political office. However, now we aren't graced by positive externalities.

This election, the notable novel events are all negative. Very negative. Never before have both party nominees boasted such low approval ratings. Never before have they been so heavily investigated (Hillary literally, Trump's investigation is more about his words past and present). Never before have the media been so intentionally caustic. Never before have outrageous lies been told. Typical to Presidential candidates is usually a specific kind of lying. Picking numbers, taking studies out of context, and bending truths to fit their platform. But never before have we seen such outrageous lies on both sides with the sole purpose of public appeal.

Most importantly, never have the constituents been so let down.

The media hasn't sought truths, and the coverage we've seen is really no better than TMZ, as I mentioned a few weeks prior. If we want things to change, let's work to not support the system that is in place. This election has been blessed by technology, and will still be tomorrow. If you don't own cable, or don't want to watch very overdone coverage, here are some options for you this election cycle:

Facebook's elections tool is backended by Center for Technology and Civic Life, nonpartisan groups aiming to give everyone knowledge on where they can go to vote and what will be on their ballot. Facebook has also teamed up with news outlets, like NowThis, to live stream election coverage on their page. CNN, the Washington Post, New York Times, Univision, PBS NewsHour, the Daily Caller, ABC News, and Vox all have streaming plans, as well. This will start as early as 4 p.m. MST.

Twitch and Twitter are teaming up with BuzzFeed to stream live election results from BuzzFeed's New York headquarters. Coverage will begin around 4 p.m. MST on election.twitter.com.

YouTube has teamed up with various news outlets, providing livestreams of coverage. The teams streaming include NBC News, PBS, Telemundo, MTV News, Bloomberg, and The Young Turks.

Snapchat will also provide coverage in the quintessential Snapchat way -- the discover tab. Make sure to look at their local stories, national stories, and discover tab for snippets of fellow voters going through the voting process. Not to mention those filters coming we're all going to love.

Finally, some real news about this election. The DoJ has sent fewer field agents out into the nation this election cycle. The agents are responsible for going to voting hubs to ensure there are no discriminatory practices and that everyone who is eligible to vote, can. Since there's fewer of them, things might slip through the cracks. Be weary! So, complaints of possible voting violations can be reported to the Civil Rights Division by calling: 1-800-253-3931; 202-307-2767 or 202-305-0082. Reports also can be made by fax to 202-307-3961, by email to voting.section@usdoj.gov and on the department’s website: www.justice.gov/crt/votercomplaint.
